Console

Redesigning Ascendion’s enterprise platform for building and governing AI agents

The agent-building tools split into two camps: powerful but developer-only, or easy but unfit for enterprise. None of them had governance. Console had to be all three at once.

Project
Enterprise Console, Ascendion · Mar 2025 – present
My role

Team
Four designers, director reviewing
Outcome
Adoption +50%, 1,000+ users at one client
Status
Shipped · V2 done · ongoing

The situation

Ascendion had already built an agentic platform. Almost nobody was using it.

Version one let enterprise teams create AI agents and chain them into workflows. It shipped and stalled. Two things were wrong at once: the agent technology wasn't producing good enough output, and the interface didn't let people build what they came to build.

That mattered commercially. Ascendion's clients wanted AI inside their workflows and had no idea how to implement it, so the company sold them a platform for building agents against their own processes. A product with weak adoption can't be sold that way.

The rebuild was funded on two fronts: strengthen the technology, redesign the experience. Design owned half of a commercial bet.

Who actually uses this

Five roles, all building for themselves.

Developers, DevOps, QE engineers, engineering managers and product managers. A QE engineer builds an agent that writes user stories; a PM builds one that drafts PRDs. Developers build for the SDLC, but not as one thing.

The observation that shaped the product

The full SDLC is too large to be one pipeline, so developers chain small agents instead: take the design from a classifier agent, arrange the work in VS Code, push to GitHub, each passing to the next.

That set the unit of the product. What a user builds isn't a workflow. It's an agent that composes with other agents.

What was actually broken

The audit named four problems. Two of them were the same problem.

Old Console put thirteen items in one flat side panel: Prompts, Models, Collections, Tools, Agents, Workflows, Guardrails, Analytics, Security, Plugins, Settings, Playground, ChatBot Config.

Almost every one is a type of object: thirteen equal choices, with nothing saying which you needed, in what order, or which existed only to serve the others.

The clearest instance

Create a guardrail inside an agent and it lived in one tab. Attaching that guardrail to the same agent happened in another.

One task, split across the interface. Users got lost inside a job they had already started.

That one fracture accounts for both of the first two findings. "Inconsistent UI" and "difficult navigation" were one structural failure described from two angles. Naming it mattered: the fix wasn't visual cleanup, it was consolidation.

What I didn't know going in

Three sharply different native habitats, and no obvious principle to collapse them.

  • Developers live in VS Code, with resizable panes, several sections open at once, and a layout they arrange themselves
  • DevOps engineers want something terminal-shaped, because that is the surface they trust
  • Product managers want step by step, because their risk is losing the thread of a process

Console is configurable enough that almost anyone can build for their own use, and people outside these roles do. But these were the habits we designed against, alongside engineering managers. A separate interface for each was never viable, and no obvious principle collapsed them into one. We went in without it.

Honest about the answer

What we landed on, that every persona converges on text, was a bet rather than a validated finding. It held up in adoption, but it wasn't proven before we committed.

Day zero: the thing nobody had

Two camps, one tradeoff, and governance missing from all of it.

We assessed CrewAI, Metaflow, Flowise, Dify and LangGraph, with two columns most competitive audits skip: how we're better and how they're better.

  • Developer-first: CrewAI, LangGraph, Flowise. Real orchestration power, a high learning curve, and in CrewAI's case no visual pipeline at all.
  • Simplicity-first: Dify, Metaflow. Very easy to use, with limited control and limited enterprise scale.

One axis, one tradeoff: power or accessibility, pick one. Nobody occupied the corner where both were true.

The more useful finding was in the weakness column

Flowise: "no governance or marketplace." Dify: "limited enterprise scale." Metaflow: "limited control & scalability."

Nobody had governance. Developer-first tools assumed the builder was technical and trusted; no-code tools assumed the stakes were low. Neither survives an enterprise, where the person building an agent is often not the person accountable for it.

The team organised around two ideas: day zero, a user arriving with a process specific to their organisation and needing to build from nothing, and governance, so that freedom is survivable at scale.

At enterprise level you have to give users the freedom to build what they need. Not a version of what someone else needed.

Hand people primitives and total freedom, and you have also handed them the ability to build something unsafe, unreviewed and unrepeatable, inside a company that will be held responsible for it. That tension is the design problem.

Two things worth saying out loud

The most accurate diagnosis had the least power. Engineers named the actual failures and sit at low influence, P2.

The highest-priority concern was a perception. "Perceived slow workflow" is the honest phrasing, and it's the P0.

And they connect. "Engineers" here are the people who actually use Console, the developers, DevOps and QE engineers who build agents all day. The group with the sharpest diagnosis and the least influence was the user base.

That was the job: carry their diagnosis into language the people funding the work would act on. The heat map wasn't a deliverable, it was a map of where the argument had to be won.

Decision: killing the stepper

Agent creation used to be a five-step wizard. It is now one page.

The old flow ran General Info → Prompt → Model → ICL → Guardrails, one screen at a time. That is the conventional answer to a complex configuration task, and on paper it is defensible.

In practice the audit found the opposite: five gated steps meant a long time per agent, no way to see the whole shape of what you were configuring, and, the finding that mattered most commercially, an experience hostile to non-technical users, the exact audience Console was sold to reach.

The trade

One page is denser and less forgiving at first sight. A stepper is gentler on arrival and more punishing after it: it hides the whole, makes going back expensive, and turns one task into five commitments.

We took the density. On a builder people return to constantly, that cost is paid once. A wizard charges every time.

Most case studies say "we considered a wizard." Console had one, and we removed it.

Decision: one playground

Every persona converges on text or code. So differentiate at the edge.

The obvious move is a tailored experience per role. It's also the wrong one: it multiplies surface area and fragments the product into five half-maintained things.

The persona mapping surfaced one thing: every persona converges on the same two output types. Developers want code and links. PMs want text, as a PDF, DOC or MD file.

So differentiation didn't belong in the playground. It belonged at the edge. One playground, one input model, output rendered as text, with export format the only thing that varies by role.

What it cost

Everything that made each persona distinct was given up. Developers lose the resizable multi-pane environment. PMs lose the step-by-step scaffolding that keeps them from losing the thread.

It bought a single coherent product one team could maintain and every role could learn once. On a platform sold as a general capability, coherence was worth more than fit.

Decision: the canvas gets the room

Explicitness traded for calm.

The first pass came from another designer on the team, and it was boxy: every element fully enclosed in its own rectangle.

My objection was about what the surface is for. A pipeline builder is an ideation surface, and containers that fence every element fight that.

  • Containers removed entirely; padding cut back
  • Secondary icons reduced to minimum weight
  • Roughly 65% of the screen given to the canvas
  • The agent library collapsible, so the canvas takes full width for large builds
What it cost

Boxes make an interface explicit, and removing them spends all of that. We weighed it first: containment was adding more cognitive load than it removed. The team agreed to reduce the builder to two things, the canvas and the agent library, and to accept the loss.

No difficulty was reported. Users singled out dragging agents onto the canvas as what made building fast.

The structure: grouped by verb

Thirteen peers became five intentions.

  • MySpace: where you land. Your own work.
  • Build: everything you make, agents, pipelines, APB, knowledge bases, tools, guardrails
  • Studios: separate applications the platform links out to. Entitlement-controlled: a client without the entitlement doesn't see the item at all, rather than seeing it locked.
  • Discover: the marketplace of what others published
  • Approvals: what's waiting on a decision
  • Manage: roles and access control. Admins only.
The principle

Group by the verb, not the object. The six things you can build don't compete at the top level. They nest under Build, because wanting to make something is one intention.

Thirteen peers become five intentions, and it scales: a seventh buildable type adds nothing to the navigation.

The seam I couldn't design away

Studios sit in the navigation but leave the product when clicked. A nav item that ejects you breaks the containment the rest of the structure promises.

This was an engineering constraint, not a design choice. Folding the studios into Console would have made it a very heavy application, and running them separately was the better call. It still leaves a seam I couldn't close from the design side. What I'd do about it is in the last section.

Two tiers, not six things

Guardrails, tools and knowledge bases are supporting components. Each has its own creation flow and exists to be attached to something else. Agents, pipelines and processes are what you compose from them. Build isn't a category. It's a workshop with a parts shelf.

A component doesn't go straight into circulation: build it, test it in the playground, then send it for approval. Once approved it lands in your project database, attachable to anything you build after. That's why Approvals earns a top-level place. It's the gate every reusable part passes through.

Why this is the sharper version of the story

The obvious fix for a task spanning two tabs is to put everything on one screen. That is not what happened. Creation and attachment are still separate, deliberately. The old product split them by accident, cutting a task in half. The new one separates them by layer, with a governed library between. One is fragmentation, the other is architecture.

Decision: the model belongs to the agent

A pipeline inherits its agents' models. It cannot override them.

Model configuration lives in agent creation and nowhere else. Letting a pipeline override it would have been convenient, and would have made pipelines feel more powerful.

Why it's the right constraint

Model choice is part of what an agent is. Overriding it would mean the same agent behaves differently depending on where it is used, and the whole composition model rests on an agent being a known quantity you can drop anywhere.

Every tier owns its own properties and higher tiers inherit rather than override. That's what keeps a reusable part reusable.

Console is deployed across multiple enterprise clients. At the one I worked closest to it reached 1,000+ users.

Where that number comes from

Our senior director reported it, measured across the year after deployment. I don't have visibility into how it was calculated, which is worth saying rather than presenting it as my own.

What users named specifically:

  • Ease of agent and pipeline creation: one screen for each, rather than a configuration journey
  • The build-and-test model: make it, run it in the playground, adjust
  • Drag-and-drop composition from the library onto the canvas, which they described as what made building quick

What didn't work

Agent output quality fell short, and part of that was a design problem.

Users had to supply far more context than expected before an agent returned anything usable, and revise repeatedly to get there. The root cause sat in the model layer, not the interface.

But the design half is mine to own

If users must supply more context to get good output, then the surface where they supply it is a design surface. Context scaffolding, worked examples, inline guidance on what a given agent needs: all interface work that measurably changes output quality.

Blaming the model alone would be accurate but incomplete.

V2: describe it instead of configuring it

Version two added natural-language creation across every one of the six build types.

Instead of filling a form, a user describes what they want and it gets built. The form-based path stayed; the prompt became the faster route in.

Why this is the right answer to the previous section

The V1 complaint was that agents needed far more context than users expected to give. A form is a poor instrument for that: it asks for values, not intent.

A prompt is a context-shaped surface. Letting someone say what they are trying to achieve, in their own words, gathers exactly what the model was starving for. The fix for a model problem turned out to be an interface.

What I'd do differently

I'd make it as simple as ChatGPT.

One prompt box. You describe what you want (an agent, a pipeline, a process, a guardrail, a tool, a knowledge base) and it gets built. The process assembles itself above the prompt box as it happens, so you watch the thing take shape rather than waiting for it. You pick your project, or drop into a particular studio, from the same place.

Which puts a question mark over my own best work

If one prompt box can create any of the six build types, the verb-grouped navigation I'm proudest of matters less. You don't need a parts shelf if you can just ask for the part.

Both are true: that structure was right for the product as it existed, and a prompt-first product wouldn't need as much of it. V2 was the first step, and it isn't finished.

The seam in the navigation

Studios still open in a new tab, and I understand why: Console would be too heavy otherwise. But users don't experience an architecture decision. They experience a nav item that ejects them. Given it again, I'd argue for signalling the jump rather than letting it look like every other destination in the panel.

And the part that's genuinely unfinished

Accessibility is the weakest area and I ranked it last. The design system's reusable components still don't meet the standard.

There's a structural reason, though not an excuse. An agentic platform needs components the design system has no patterns for: streaming output, confidence and provenance, human-review states. Building them has meant breaking our own rules rather than extending the system. Fixing it means working at the system level, not the screen level, and that hasn't happened yet.

Questions about any of these decisions?

The parts I’d most like to be pushed on are the day-zero bet and the output model. Both were judgement calls, and I can show the working.
